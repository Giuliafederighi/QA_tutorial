Understanding AI Training and the Q/A Role

Many people enter the project thinking they will train the AI directly — with full focus on the assistant. Those who have been Taskers before tend to arrive more familiar, since they were the ones talking to the assistant. It's important to correct this view from the start.

🎭 Think Like a Theater Director

The Q/A doesn't evaluate what the AI said. The Q/A evaluates the Tasker's performance — like a director behind the scenes. You check if the actor followed the script, stayed in character, and delivered a convincing scene.

The Characters in the Process

🎯

The Task

Has a well-defined training objective: exposing the AI to real-life situations that seem simple but hide complexity. The same scenario can change completely depending on the factors involved.

🎬

The Tasker (Actor)

Is an expert in the task's subject area. Their role is to simulate the scenario authentically: they read the script (Scenario Context), understand their role (User Persona), and conduct the conversation with the assistant naturally.

🤖

The Assistant (AI)

Is the other side of the conversation — the one being tested. But note: you do not evaluate the quality of the AI's responses. You evaluate whether the Tasker performed well.

🎓

The Q/A (You)

Is the director. You systematically review the Tasker's work, check if mistakes were made, and decide whether the task can be approved, needs correction, or should be rejected.

Why the Simulation Exists

For the AI to be ready to handle complex real-life scenarios, they need to be tested — preferably in a controlled environment. Ideally, the same task is simulated more than once, with different actors, since they will always perform in distinct ways. But the intent is the same: learning.

What Makes Up a Task

  • Scenario Context — The task's objective. Sometimes includes instructions like "start this way", "then talk about X", "once the persona reveals Y, focus on Z".
  • User Persona — The character the Tasker must portray.
  • Initial Prompt — The first message sent to the assistant.
  • Hidden Context — Information the Tasker knows but cannot reveal upfront — it must emerge naturally throughout the conversation.
  • Evaluation Criteria — Expected performance parameters for the AI. These are specific conditions to determine whether the AI passed or failed each criterion.
💡 The Core Idea

You review the human's work, not the assistant's. The quality of the AI's responses is a result of training — what you ensure is that the input data (the Tasker's conversation) was well executed.

Function Overview & the Platform

🎭 The Central Action: Conduct Conversation

Within each task, the Conduct Conversation action is where the play truly happens. This is where the Tasker steps on stage — takes on the persona, reads the script (Scenario Context + Hidden Context), and conducts the conversation with the assistant as if they were a real person in that situation.

Think of it this way: if the task is the screenplay, the Conduct Conversation is the filming. Without a good filming, the screenplay is worthless.

Minimum of 5 conversation rounds. Just as a scene that is too short doesn't tell a complete story, a conversation with fewer than 5 exchanges rarely develops the narrative enough to be useful for training. The minimum of 5 rounds is the minimum scene length for the story to make sense.

How to Get New Tasks

Tasks are assigned via auto-assign on the platform. If you have any problem or question about a specific task, send it to the Slack channel or reach out directly to someone on the team.

⚠️ Correct Filters — Always

Apply Status: in review first, then oldest first. In the wrong order, older tasks may not appear and the queue breaks.

What Belongs to the Task vs. the Tasker

This distinction is critical for correctly identifying AI use. Many beginner Q/As go looking for em-dashes everywhere — but the em-dash may be in the task's pre-defined content, not from the Tasker.

📋 Task Content (pre-defined)

  • User Persona
  • Scenario Context
  • Initial Prompt (sometimes pre-defined)
  • Hidden Context
  • Evaluation criteria descriptions

✍️ Tasker Content (written by the human)

  • Initial Prompt (if modified or written from scratch)
  • All messages in the conversation with the assistant
  • Pass/fail justifications for the criteria
🔍 How to check if the Initial Prompt was edited

Use the task history (History) to see if the Tasker modified the pre-defined Initial Prompt or created a new one. This is important for understanding whether the content is original from the Tasker or from the task.

Technical Situations and What to Do

Case Task with no conversation
Go to the task's three-dot menu and use the Report option. Do not try to correct or approve a task with no conversation — report it to the team.
Case Task with no evaluation criteria answered
Same action — use the three-dot menu → Report. Note the problem clearly in the description field.
Case Tasker commented they don't want to correct
Use the three-dot menu → Report and escalate to the team. Document the Tasker's comment. Do not force approval of a task that doesn't meet the standards.

Characteristics of Good Feedback

Regardless of the decision (approved, needs correction, or rejection), your feedback must be specific, actionable, and evidence-based. See the differences:

✗ Generic: "The prompt has issues, please correct it."
✓ Specific: "The Initial Prompt directly mentions the $50k debt — this is part of the Hidden Context. Remove the reference to the specific amount and rewrite so the AI discovers this information naturally throughout the conversation."

If the Tasker made a mistake

Be specific: cite the message, field, or exact part where the error occurred. Don't use canned or generic phrases — incomplete feedback can make the Tasker redo everything and still need further corrections.

If it's a suggested improvement

Even in approved tasks, guide when relevant: "be careful not to leave the conversation without a proper closing", "remember the idea is not to give hints to the assistant".

✓ Praise when deserved

If you find something excellent, say so. This motivates the Tasker to keep up the good work and reinforces what should be repeated.

Note about pre-defined Initial Prompt

If you notice the Tasker started with the task's pre-defined prompt (which frequently leaks information — not always critically), you can comment like this:

"The conversation started with the task's pre-defined prompt; while this is not prohibited, please ensure it aligns with the task's purpose. Remember that as a reviewer, we expect you to verify if the initial prompt is consistent with the persona and scenario, and that it contains no significant hidden context leakage."

Note: If you're not sure whether it's the pre-defined initial prompt, don't comment on it.

Q/A Step-by-Step — How to Systematize a Review

  • 1
    Familiarize yourself with the task

    Check if the Tasker left any comments about a specific difficulty, or if there is already feedback from a previous correction. If there is, the task is coming back from a correction — read the history carefully (see Step 5).

  • 2
    Check the Initial Prompt

    Is it natural for the persona? Are there critical Hidden Context leaks? Compare the Initial Prompt in the Scenario Details tab with the one in the conversation with the assistant. If they differ and you're not sure what happened, ask for help in the channel.

  • 3
    Evaluate the conversation

    Read all turns before judging anything. See the criteria below.

  • 4
    Evaluate the evaluation criteria

    Check whether the PASS/FAIL decisions make sense and whether the justifications are evidence-based.

What is Critical Hidden Context Leakage?

It is the leakage of Hidden Context that makes the conversation too easy, with no challenge at all. How to identify it: the conversation usually doesn't flow and the user keeps trying to develop something that goes nowhere.

If you want a more objective criterion: do not reveal more than 2 hard constraints in the Initial Prompt. Rewriting the Hidden Context is also considered leakage.

🔪 The Knife Rule

Never reveal the knife (the central element of the Hidden Context), not even part of it, nor hint at it in the Initial Prompt.

Conversation Evaluation Criteria

Check Is the conversation entirely in English?
The Tasker's messages must be in English. Any mixing of languages is a red flag.
Check Is the conversation natural?
Do the messages sound like a real person? Casual language, appropriate to the persona, without robotic or excessively polished patterns.
Check Did the Tasker maintain the persona until the end?
Sometimes the Tasker slips and starts speaking in the third person, or acts as if they are correcting the AI. In a real conversation, you don't get confused about who you are.
Check Does the Tasker interact with the assistant or just dump information?
The idea is not to give hints to the assistant, not to make things artificially difficult, but also not to make them too easy. Information should be revealed organically — usually by relating to the last message sent by the assistant.

If it seems like new information is being added without context, or that the Tasker is guiding the AI toward a specific answer, that's a problem.
Check Is there clear AI use?
This will be covered in detail in Step 4. For now, red flags include: excessively polished and templated language, consistently formatted structure across all turns, presence of commands to another AI or excerpts from conversations with another AI.

Important: polished grammar alone is not sufficient to accuse someone of AI use. But you can mention to the Tasker that very formal grammar and excess of formal punctuation may not feel natural and could suggest external assistance.
Check Was the knife revealed at the right moment?
The knife should be revealed IF:
  • The assistant deserves it — by asking, referencing the related topic, or creating a natural hook for it
  • It is natural for that persona to share it at that moment
If the Tasker missed a clear opportunity to reveal it, that can be a Needs Correction. But if the fault of not revealing lies with the assistant (who never asked), it's not a problem.

Ideally it should be revealed starting from the third message sent. But if it's extremely natural before that, it can be weighed accordingly.
Check Was the real minimum number of messages reached?
The minimum is 14 exchanged messages (including the Initial Prompt). But what matters is that the messages have content.

If the end is filled with multiple "thank you"s, several isolated points, or messages with no real content — that doesn't count as a developed conversation. One "thank you" at the end is acceptable, more than one is not.
Check Was the conversation concluded?
Not all conversations need a solid conclusion. But the story should not be cut halfway just because the minimum message count was reached.

You can leave it as feedback to the Tasker that they feel free to explore the story further — as long as it's done naturally, without pressure from the minimum.

Evaluating the Evaluation Criteria

Do you agree with the PASS/FAIL decisions? We typically look for more critical points — when the AI clearly did not do what was described and still got a PASS, or vice versa. If the criterion is subjective and the justification is plausible but you disagree, you don't need to send it for correction — value the Tasker's opinion (who generally has domain expertise).

Ideal justification

Passed: In turn 5, the AI asked about income stability. In turn 7, it probed monthly expenses. By turn 9, it synthesized both into a tailored savings plan — directly addressing the 'financial assessment' criterion.

Good justification (without turn references)

If it's well explained but lacks turn references, leave it as a suggestion for them to start referencing in future tasks — don't send it for correction.

⚠️ Generic justification = Needs Correction

If it's generic ("the AI was helpful", "the conversation was natural"), request a correction and guide them to bring specific turn references.

The Final Decision and What Counts as Clear AI Use

The approval threshold was adjusted to avoid unnecessary bottlenecks. We moved from a filter where only "Good" would pass to also accepting "Medium-Good". See the categories:

✓ Good

"Yes, absolutely." Everything is correct, natural, and well evaluated. Immediate approval.

~ Medium-Good

Not a clear "yes", but not a "no" either. Usually has 1–2 "Yes" and some "Partially" in the Q/A steps. Can be approved with feedback.

! Medium-Bad

Structural or logic problems. Needs correction.

✗ Bad

Serious problems. Depending on the cause, Needs Correction or Reject.

Decision Criteria: Approve vs. Correct

If the task is Medium-Good, use the following logic:

→ Approve

The correction would be costly — to fix a detail, the Tasker would have to redo nearly everything. In this case, approve with guiding feedback.

→ Needs Correction

It's a Quick Fix — just continuing the conversation for one more turn, or correcting a misapplied evaluation criterion. The correction request is worth it.

📌 Importance Hierarchy

Persona interpretation ≥ Naturalness and conversation flow > Good use of hidden context in the conversation / no critical leaks in the initial prompt.

When to Reject

  • Repeated correction requests (individualized — assess the history)
  • Clear AI use: presence of commands to another AI, excerpts from conversations with another AI
🚨 What Counts as Clear AI Use

It's not just polished grammar. Clear use includes: commands to another AI visible in the text, excerpts from conversations with ChatGPT or similar, extremely templated and consistent language across ALL turns with no human variation. Polished grammar alone is not sufficient to accuse someone of AI use.

Still Unsure?

💬 Doubt Protocol

Send the task link in the Slack channel and ask for guidance on how to proceed. It's always better to ask than to make the wrong decision.

Q/A After Needs Correction

When a task comes back from a correction, the review process is different. You need to verify not only the current quality, but whether the previous feedback was properly applied.

  • 1
    Check the last feedback given

    Read the previous correction comment before anything else. What was requested? Was it to change the Initial Prompt? Continue the conversation? Fix criteria?

  • 2
    Check if the Tasker left a comment

    Sometimes the Tasker comments that they didn't correct for a specific reason (example: "I don't know how to run a new conversation with the assistant"). This changes how you should proceed.

  • 3
    Check the change history

    If the feedback was to change the Initial Prompt, the history should show that change and a new conversation with the assistant. Always compare both Initial Prompts to confirm that a new conversation was run.

  • 4
    Note: not everything appears in the history

    The history doesn't always record all changes made. But Initial Prompt changes are usually recorded. Use this information carefully.

⚠️ If you disagree with previous feedback

Do your own individual analysis and give your new verdict, without exposing your colleague in the task comment. If the previous feedback had a significant error, contact the team privately and share it discreetly.

🎯 Goal of Step 5

Ensure that the correction was genuine — not just that the task looks better, but that the requested changes were actually implemented. A task that "looks ok" but was not corrected as requested should still be sent back.
